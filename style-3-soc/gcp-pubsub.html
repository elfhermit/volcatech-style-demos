Pub/Sub

Google Cloud · Messaging

A messaging service for events that move between systems. Publishers and subscribers never address each other directly, so either side can be replaced without a coordinated release.

02At-least-once delivery Every message is delivered at least once, so subscribers must be written to handle a repeat.

Contact us All analytics services

The problem

What teams run into without it.

Point-to-point integrations

Every system talks directly to every other system it needs. Each new consumer means another bespoke connection, and one slow endpoint can stall the producers that call it.

Tight release coupling

Producers and consumers must change in lockstep because they share interfaces and availability. A deployment on one side forces coordination meetings on the other.

Event loss under load

Traffic spikes overwhelm downstream services with no buffer between them. Events are dropped or retried ad hoc, and nobody can say afterwards what was actually processed.

Why teams choose it

What Pub/Sub is good at.

Producers and consumers decoupled

A publisher writes to a topic and is done; subscribers read at their own pace. Neither side needs the other to be running at the moment the event occurs.

Throughput without capacity planning

The service absorbs bursts of publishing without a queue to size in advance. Load that arrives unevenly does not have to be smoothed by the publisher.

One endpoint, many regions

Publishers and subscribers use the same global endpoint wherever they run. Routing between regions is handled by the service rather than by your own topology.

Key features

What you get, specifically.

01

Global endpoint

A single addressable endpoint serves publishers and subscribers in any region, with no per-region topic to route between.

03

Dataflow integration

Topics connect directly to Dataflow pipelines, so streaming transformation does not need a bridging component of its own.

04

Availability commitment

Google's SLA for Pub/Sub commits to a monthly uptime percentage of at least 99.95%.

05

Scale and limits

A single Pub/Sub message can carry up to 10 MB of payload data.

06

Message retention

Unacknowledged messages are held in a subscription for seven days by default, and the window can be set anywhere from 10 minutes to 31 days.

07

Schema on the topic

A topic can carry an Avro or protocol buffer schema, and a message that does not match it is refused at publish rather than at the subscriber.

Where it fits

Three situations it is built for.

IoT telemetry

Readings from devices in the field arrive continuously and are buffered until a pipeline is ready to process them. Retention keeps that buffer available for a set window, so a consumer taken down for maintenance catches up afterwards instead of losing readings.

Log pipelines

Application and infrastructure logs collected from many sources and forwarded into analysis without each source knowing the destination.

Event notification

State changes in one service announced once and consumed by whichever other services currently care about them.

Choosing well

When Pub/Sub is the right call — and when it is not.

  • Decoupling services with asynchronous event delivery

    →Pub/Sub

  • Transforming or aggregating streams before they land

    →Dataflow

  • Querying events after they have landed

    →BigQuery

  • Exposing synchronous request-response APIs to consumers

    →API Gateway

Common questions

What evaluators ask.

01Can messages be kept within European regions?

Yes. Message storage policies restrict where Pub/Sub stores message data to the regions you list, such as EU regions, and the platform enforces the policy rather than relying on client configuration.

02What delivery guarantees does Pub/Sub provide?

Delivery is at-least-once by default, so consumers must handle occasional duplicates. Exactly-once delivery can be enabled on subscriptions within a region, and dead-letter topics catch messages that repeatedly fail.

03Does Pub/Sub preserve message order?

Not by default: subscribers may receive messages out of order. Publishing with an ordering key delivers messages for that key in the order they were sent, within a region.

04Do we need Dataflow as well as Pub/Sub?

Not always. Pub/Sub moves events; Dataflow processes them. If subscribers only need each message as it is, Pub/Sub alone is enough. Add Dataflow when streams need transformation, enrichment or windowed aggregation.

05What drives Pub/Sub costs?

Throughput — the volume of data published and delivered — plus retained message storage and traffic between regions. Actual cost depends on message volumes and architecture; contact us for an estimate based on your workloads.

06How do subscribers actually receive messages?

Three ways, chosen per subscription. A pull subscription has your client ask for messages when it is ready; a push subscription has Pub/Sub post them to an HTTPS endpoint you expose; and an export subscription writes them straight into BigQuery or Cloud Storage with no consumer of your own in between. One topic can carry several subscriptions at once, so a service that pulls and an export that lands the same events in the warehouse are not alternatives — they run side by side.

07Can we replay messages after a bad release?

Yes, within the retention window. Seeking a subscription back to a timestamp makes everything published after that moment eligible for delivery again, so a consumer that processed events incorrectly can reprocess them once it is fixed. A snapshot captures the acknowledgement state of a subscription before a risky change and can be returned to later; snapshots last at most seven days, and a seek can take up to a minute to take effect.

08Can one consumer take only part of a topic?

Yes. A subscription can carry a filter over message attributes, and only the messages matching it are delivered; the rest are acknowledged by the service itself and never reach your subscriber. Filters are fixed when the subscription is created, so changing the rule means creating a replacement subscription rather than editing the existing one.
